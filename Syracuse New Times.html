    How Syracuse schools, churches, and youth teams can run simpler fall and winter fundraisers

    Most fundraiser committees do not fail because people are stingy. They fail when a well-meaning parent takes on a job that quietly eats 40 hours between Halloween and Christmas. The fix is usually not more enthusiasm. It is picking a format that matches the season and the number of volunteers you actually have.

    For groups in Syracuse and across Central New York, a simple approach works better: choose one product or event, keep the selling window to about two weeks, and do not run three campaigns at once. A single focused push that leaves the committee intact in January beats several scattered efforts.

    Syracuse Unified and the surrounding districts cover dense city neighbABC Fundraising orhoods, suburban towns, and more rural areas farther out. A fundraiser that works at one school can stall at another because of travel distance, weather, and how far families will go on an icy night. Plan for that difference instead of treating every group the same.

    What is different about fall and winter here

    Daylight shrinks after the clocks change. Door-to-door selling on a Tuesday evening in November is a harder ask than it was in September. Parents also face holiday spending, school concerts, basketball, and church events in the same window.

    A practical calendar for this region:

    • Fall campaigns in late September and October, before Halloween.
    • Winter campaigns in January and early February, after the holidays and before spring sports.
    • Late November and December are usually weak for product sales and better for a plain donation ask if the purpose is clear.

    The Internal Revenue Service publishes plain-language guidance on what donors can and cannot deduct. Groups should check that before promising anything on a flyer.

    Logistics matter just as much. A group that draws from a wide area needs delivery built into the plan. A group in a tight city neighborhood can often hand products out at a single pickup night and skip shipping. That one fact does more to choose the format than a profit-percentage chart.

    Pick the lane before the product

    Committees often spend a whole meeting debating popcorn versus cookie dough. Decide the lane first.

    • School lane. Elementary PTAs usually need volume without chaos. Order forms, one distribution night, and one shipment. Cookie dough and packaged snacks fit here.
    • Church lane. Congregations respond better to a gathering than door-knocking. A supper or coffee hour after service does more work. Product sales are a backup.
    • Youth team lane. Parents are already at the same gym or rink. Keep the campaign to about two weeks and put the heaviest push on game nights.
    • Middle and high school lane. Older students can share an online store link. Parents handle pickup. Keep meetings short.
    • Holiday lane. Poinsettias, wreaths, or seasonal items can work in November and December when other selling is hard.

    Run two lanes at most. Three usually means one fails and the blame lands on whoever had the least free time.

    Programs such as those from ABC Fundraising are one option for groups that want a set product, brochures, and an online store instead of building the process from scratch.

    A fall and winter calendar that does not collide with itself

    Write fixed dates first: homecoming, the school musical, the church harvest supper, basketball openers, finals week. Then put fundraising windows in the gaps.

    Window

    Best fit

    Skip it if

    Late September to mid-October

    School order-form campaigns and youth team sales

    Homecoming week falls inside it

    Late October to Thanksgiving

    Church suppers, donation drives, plain asks

    The committee is already thin

    December

    Holiday greens, poinsettias, wreath sales

    Pickup space and delivery are not lined up

    January to mid-February

    Frozen food, coffee, or a spring kickoff

    Weather could cancel distribution

    Build in one weather contingency for anything that requires people to show up. That is normal for Central New York winters.

    Which format uses the fewest volunteer hours

    Online stores win on time. A seller shares a link, orders ship or arrive in one delivery, and the committee focuses on promotion instead of sorting boxes. The Bureau of Labor Statistics has long tracked volunteering rates, and many families are already stretched across several causes. Do not ask for more hours than the campaign needs.

    Order-form fundraisers still work in neighborhoods where families see each other at pickup. Someone has to receive the shipment, sort it, and staff the night. That cost is easy to underestimate.

    Donation drives win on simplicity. No product, no delivery, no returns. For a church group or a small team, a clear ask can raise as much as a product push and leave volunteers with a weekend free.

    If a group has fewer than six committed volunteers, skip the order-form fundraiser. Run an online store or a donation drive. You may earn less per unit and more per volunteer hour, and the committee is more likely to do it again next year.

    What makes distribution day go sideways

    Distribution is where fall and winter campaigns fall apart. Frozen items thawing in a warm car. A truck arriving two days early. A custodian who did not know the gym was reserved. Many districts, including Syracuse City School District, require facility-use approvals and sometimes custodial coverage. Confirm the space and the delivery window in writing before you order.

    Freeze the seller list before you place the order. Late additions create shortages. Early dropouts create leftovers. If the product is perishable, count carefully and keep a small cushion.

    Five-step checklist

    1. Pick one lane and one backup format. Write both down at the first meeting.
    2. Block a two-week selling window and put it on the school or church calendar.
    3. Confirm pickup date, space, and delivery window in writing before you order.
    4. Set one clear dollar goal and say what the money is for.
    5. Write a one-page wrap-up the week after distribution and save it for next year’s chair.

    Closing

    Simple fundraisers in Syracuse and Central New York come down to three decisions: the lane, the two-week window, and the volunteers you refuse to burn out. Get those right and the product matters less. Get them wrong and even an easy catalog turns into a February full of texts nobody wants.

    Before the next meeting, put one question on the agenda: which campaign can this group honestly run this season?
